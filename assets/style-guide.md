# The style guide

## Principles

1. Be direct with the information you want to communicate. Don't be roundabout.
2. Use the active voice.
3. One idea per sentence.
4. Affirmative is better than negative.
5. Write words people like. Verbs are better than nouns. Nouns are better than adjectives and adverbs.
6. Avoid similes and metaphors. When explaining a complex idea, break it down into simple ideas.
7. Avoid new words as they will likely be short-lived, and avoid complex nouns formed by adding prefixes and suffixes to verbs.
8. Choose substance over style, even if it contradicts any of these rules.

## Word choice

Attack nominalization. Words like "initialization" should not be part of text you edit. Rewrite them using the verb they nominalized, like "start".

No hedging. Whatever the topic, avoid hedging language like "may", "might", "could", "seems", "appears". Be direct and assertive and list specific counterexamples only when strictly necessary. Hedging makes writing less persuasive, and most writing is trying to convince someone of something.

Gendered pronouns for a generic person: "he/she" and "him/her" are unsayable aloud. If the person is known, name them. If the person is generic, use the plural: "When each student receives the exam, he/she may not leave" becomes "After receiving their exams, students may not leave".

## Sentence structure

Avoid contrasts saying both what something is and isn't. Rewrite these patterns:

1. "X is not just A, but also B": "He is not just a teacher, but also a coach" becomes "He is a teacher and a coach".
2. "X is not A, but B": "The thing that blows up is not the deficit; it's the debt" becomes "But it's the debt that blows up".
3. "X is A, not B": "It reads as an encyclopedia entry, not a story" becomes "It reads as an encyclopedia entry".

Don't use bursts of three everywhere. Where three actions, benefits or characteristics come in a short sequence ("fast, reliable, and scalable"; "plan, execute, and optimize"), keep only the key one.

Don't append adjectives as a delimited phrase after the noun. "Frontal passage doesn't just rotate the wind, it usually punches it up — gusty, stronger, and often drier behind the front" becomes "Frontal passage rotates the wind and often makes it gusty."

Don't end sentences with a trailing gerund phrase presenting a consequence. "Automation massively cuts down costs, enabling teams to scale" becomes "Automation helps teams scale by cutting costs massively". "The measure brought little improvement in living conditions, highlighting the need for a more nuanced approach" becomes "The measure failed to improve living conditions and we need something better".

Vary sentence length and structure. Constantly using long sentences and repeating the same patterns makes text monotonous. Follow up a long sentence with a short one.

Remove absolute phrases: "We scrambled along the shore, the waves splashing at our feet" becomes "The waves splashed at our feet as we scrambled along the shore."

Remove em dashes and en dashes. Use full stops or commas to delimit clauses or asides.

## Document structure

Avoid lists and list-like paragraphs. Don't use headings or bold around one phrase to introduce each "key concept". Human writing is lumpy: some ideas need several paragraphs, others one sentence at the end of another paragraph.

Avoid the rigid "topic sentence, elaboration, example, wrap-up" pattern for every paragraph. Pick the structure that fits each case.

Don't balance every argument with a counterexample just to look balanced. Drive the point, include only defenses against the most likely attacks.
